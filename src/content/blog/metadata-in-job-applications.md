In a job application every line is read three times before it goes out. The file itself hardly anyone reads — yet it often says more than you meant to show. None of it is a disaster, but some of it is needlessly awkward, and some of it is simply private.

## The usual suspects

**The title of your last application.** Word keeps the document title unchanged when you copy a file. Use the cover letter for company A as a template for company B and you often end up sending a PDF whose title still reads "Cover letter company A". Some PDF viewers show exactly that title in the window bar — not the file name.

**Someone else's name as author.** CV templates from the web, from friends or from a careers workshop carry the name of whoever created them originally. The author field is not replaced automatically when you keep editing.

**Comments from the proofreading round.** If someone reviews your cover letter, it often comes back with comments and suggested changes. Accepted changes and deleted comments disappear from view, but not necessarily from the file — and depending on the export settings, markup can end up in the PDF.

**Editing time and timestamps.** Office files keep count of how long they were worked on and how often they were saved. A creation date three years before the application shows that your CV has a long history.

**Internal file paths.** The archive files inside Word documents hold references to linked files, often with user names and folder names like "Applications/Rejected". More on this in [Removing metadata from PDF and Word](/en/blog/remove-metadata-from-pdf-and-word/).

**The application photo.** A studio photo carries camera data, capture date and often the photographer's copyright details. A photo from your own phone can also contain the capture location — possibly your home. Depending on the app, that data survives when the image is embedded in the document.

## Who sees any of this

Hardly anyone in HR deliberately opens the document properties. But the information turns up in places you would not expect: as the window title in the PDF viewer, in file previews, when several attachments are merged into one file. And applicant tracking systems parse uploaded files automatically to pre-fill fields — title and author get processed along the way.

Checking beforehand takes little effort. The payoff is that your application shows exactly what you want it to show.

## Checklist before you send

1. **Check the file name.** "CoverLetter_Lastname_Company.pdf" rather than "cover_letter_final_v3_companyA.pdf".
2. **Resolve tracked changes.** Accept or reject every change, delete every comment — before exporting.
3. **Check title and author in the Word document.** File → Info shows the most important fields.
4. **Check the finished PDF, not just the Word document.** The export writes new metadata, so what counts in the end is what is in the PDF.
5. **Look at attachments individually.** References, certificates and work samples come from other sources and carry their traces.
6. **Clean the final files.** That is the one step that backs up all the others.

Application documents often carry the title of an earlier application, someone else's name as author, comments from proofreading and the metadata of the application photo.
